Overview

Two questions per departure, then a chain of corrections.

Is the record a copy of the schedule, and if not, how long did the aircraft taxi? The prediction mixes the two answers by the copy probability p; rules, models that read the open ground track and a published daily total then correct it.

Hover a box to see what it reads, click it to jump to its section; the line underneath is the error after each box. The reference chain shown here also calibrates to a monthly taxi-out per runway; the shipped chain does not.

The label measures the move from stand to runway, if the record is honest.

In about {{phys_rows}} % of departures the record is an event: someone saw the aircraft push back. In the rest ({{copy_share_2025}} of 2025, {{copy_month_range}} in every month, {{lirf_copy}} at Rome, LIRF) it is the scheduled time, and a flight that left three hours late has a "taxi time" of three hours. Two facts make this second process predictable: a copy's label is known exactly (take-off minus schedule, both in the test files), and arrival records are not masked, so the habits of whoever stamps the off-block show on flights whose times are visible.

The task, and the seven clocks the model reads against each other

Ten European airports, all of 2025 to learn from ({{train_rows}} labelled departures) and {{test_rows}} departures of 2026 to predict. By ICAO code: {{airport_names}}. We predict the seconds from off-block (the aircraft leaves its stand) to take-off, scored by root mean squared error (RMSE).

SCHEDthe timetable's off-block time
IOBTthe initial off-block time of the flight plan
EOBTthe estimated off-block time of the flight plan, as updated
LOBToff-block in the last flight plan filed
AOBT_3the actual off-block time as the Network Manager (NM) estimates it, independent of the airport
BLOCKthe airport's recorded off-block; hidden in the test files
MVTtake-off

AOBT_3 does most of the work: on an honest record MVT − AOBT_3 is already close to the taxi time. Learning only the correction to it cuts the error from {{baseline}} to {{anchor_to}} s, the largest single step in the descent. {{n_aobt}} of the eleven regressors start from it, the other {{n_lobt}} from LOBT.

4 · The key idea

Predict the chance that the record is a copy, then mix two answers.

The model must regress well on the many ordinary rows and recognise the kind of record on the few huge ones. It predicts the probability p of a copy and the physical taxi if not; a copy's label, MVT − SCHED, comes straight from the files. The prediction mixes them:

prediction = p · (MVT − SCHED) + (1 − p) · taxi
Why the mix is exact. The expected squared error of a guess x is p(x − A)² + (1 − p)(x − B)², a parabola whose lowest point is pA + (1 − p)B.


expected cost at this copy probabilitycost if it is a copycost if it is a physical taxi

Drag p and the delay and watch the lowest point of the blue curve. Schematic flight: a physical taxi of {{mix_phys}} minutes, or the whole delay if the record is a copy. Either answer alone costs more than the mix, most when the model is least sure.

Why 30 % must mean 30 %

If such flights are copies at rate q and the model says p, the extra cost is (p − q)² · (delay − taxi)²; a copy missed on a flight that left three hours late costs about {{miss_cost}}. So we judge the classifier by log loss, which rewards honest probabilities: {{logloss}} out of fold, against {{logloss_const}} for a constant at the base rate.

5 · Inside the boxes

What each stage reads, and where on the airport it looks.

Pick a stage to see what it reads and what it is worth, or hover a place on the airport to see which feature families look there. No input is a departure's own off-block or taxi time.

The eleven regressors, and what "out of fold" means

Each regressor starts from a clock-based guess, the anchor (take-off minus the Network Manager's off-block, or minus the plan's), and learns the correction. They differ in learner (gradient-boosted trees, neural networks, a denoising autoencoder), anchor and inputs. Alone in the mixture each scores {{member_best}}–{{member_worst}} s; the blend scores {{blend_cv}} s. Most of their value comes later, when their disagreements become an input.

Every stage learns from predictions made by a model that never saw that row's month (out of fold), so it sees errors as they look on unseen data.

6 · The ground track

The open ground track shows when the aircraft moved, which is enough.

ADS-B. Aircraft broadcast their position by radio; volunteer receivers record it.

Transponders are often off at the stand, so receivers first hear most departures after they left it (median {{first_heard}} after off-block). The track misses the off-block but shows the taxi: when the aircraft moved, where it stopped, when the take-off roll began.

Schematic track: points start only once the transponder is on; stops show as clusters; the roll start marks the runway.

A second-level model, a stacker, predicts the error the chain still makes from the track's timings, stops and path, the clock gaps and the disagreement between the eleven regressors, and adds it back. Four averaged stackers take the chain from {{rules_cv}} to {{stack_cv}} s.

Coverage is all-or-nothing by airport and changes between years, so one model corrects matched rows and another corrects unmatched rows from clocks alone. One of the four stackers weights its rows to the 2026 coverage mix.

The regressors' disagreement as an input

Each regressor's gap to the blend tells the stacker where that member is right: the track-reading members where a track exists, the plan-anchored ones where a plan exists. It gains {{disagree}}, and {{disagree_reh}} on the held-out rehearsal months.

7b · The airport as a stream

Every flight around the one being predicted.

A runway queue is a run of departures close together in the airport's stream of movements, and a model that reads one row sees one aircraft. The stream transformer reads 512 consecutive movements, departures and arrivals; each departure attends to those around it, with a learned decay per attention head. The neighbour-label head alone, against the plain track-aware member: {{nb_gain}} chain · 6 pairs, 12 months weighted.

How the network learns to work without the track, and its two members
  • Modality dropout. We hide the ADS-B block of a random share of the tracked departures, and the network must still predict them.
  • A teacher. An averaged copy of the network, the teacher, sees every track in the chunk; the student must agree with it on the departures whose track it cannot see.
  • The target months, without labels. Both read the input streams of the months they will predict, masking a share of tokens and reconstructing their inputs.

The track-aware stream member gives the stackers its prediction, its gap to the chain, its spread and its chance of a label above 30 and above 60 minutes; the shipped variant adds a head for the queue level (the neighbouring departures' mean label) and two columns. The context stream member, the same network fed the feature set's departure-stream windows, joins the eleven regressors in the disagreement features.

In the code: the track-aware member is ts_kdu3 (stx_nb with the neighbour-label head, the shipped one) and the context member stx_ctx (overnight/stx.py).

8 · The calibration

A published daily total pins down the sums, without reading any flight's label.

EUROCONTROL's daily pre-departure delay per airport equals the day's sum of max(BLOCK − SCHED, 0) (correlation {{dly_corr}} on 2025), and BLOCK = MVT − label. So the predictions imply a delay total for each day, which must match the published one. Drag the factor:

Schematic day: each bar is one departure's delay implied by its prediction; drag until their sum meets the published line.

One factor per airport and day rescales the ordinary rows until the totals match, and the step applies {{dly_apply}} of that change. It reads one number per airport-day, never a value for one flight, and is worth {{dly_gain}} s of CV RMSE on the reference chain. The organisers confirmed on 13-10-2026 that data EUROCONTROL publishes may be used.

The monthly runway taxi-out: tested and left out

The reference chain, not the shipped one, also calibrates to EUROCONTROL's mean taxi-out per airport, runway and month (valid flights only: up to 120 minutes, de-iced flights removed). It models the offset between a cell's mean label and the published mean, mostly the excluded flights, and moves the predicted rows a quarter of the way towards the published mean plus that offset, by at most {{runway_cap}} s per cell (details). It gains {{runway_gain}} s of CV RMSE [{{runway_ci}}], below the noise of one refit, though it gains in every held-out pair of months ({{runway_pairs}}); the daily step gains {{dly_gain}} s. The shipped chain leaves it out so that it uses one published aggregate of the scored months instead of two, at a cost of {{runway_cost}} RMSE on twelve months.

8b · Four small corrections

Where the stack is still off, four rules fitted on a year of out-of-fold rows.

Four post-steps, fitted on the twelve-month pool without reading a 2026 label, fix groups of rows the stack misses. Together they gained {{post_gain}} RMSE on twelve months of 2025 in an earlier version of the chain chain · 6 pairs, 12 months weighted and improve every month; about half of the gain is one far Rome record. On the two folds the chain goes from {{bundle_cv}} to {{final}}.

The four steps
  • A re-mix refits the Rome copy weight, because the chain under-commits to copying the schedule.
  • A runway-neighbour corrector subtracts the queue level that the neighbours' own predictions and clocks reveal.
  • A far-row mixture turns the few far Rome records into a blend of "copy" and "one day later", never a normal taxi; every such row in 2025 was one of the two.
  • An arrival-twin window rule reads Rome departures whose flight plan is visible only through their arrival twin.

The twelve-month gain was measured on the chain that still had the monthly runway step. Without the rows that carry most of it, the broad part is smaller (the model page).

9 · Validation

Judged on months no fitted parameter has seen.

The test months have no labels, and 2026 differs from 2025. A classifier on label-free features tells all of 2025 from January/July 2026 at {{auc_years}}, but January/July 2025 from January/July 2026 only at {{auc_same}}, so the validation folds sit on the test set's own months. Pick a scheme:

How two runs are compared

Every comparison resamples whole days 1,000 times and recomputes the difference (a day-block bootstrap), so a lucky day cannot pass as a gain. We also rehearse the whole pipeline one year early, with February and June 2025 hidden exactly like the test months, and judge the last changes on all twelve months weighted like the test set.

Using the model

Run it on new months, take an input away, add a piece.

Each stage reads its inputs by name, so the common changes are small. docs/extending.md has the details, the hardware and the time per step.

Commands for seven common changes, checked against the code

Put the new months in the layout of the two challenge files (every movement; departures' off-block and taxi time empty). Everything that reads only 2025, including the five rehearsals the month-complete stackers learn from, is reused, so a new month costs only the final phase.

# 1. the two new files in the challenge layout go to data/raw
python scripts/fetch_final.py --from-dir <dir> --refresh
# 2. data, features, member refits and the reference chain of the final phase (outputs/overnight_final)
FINAL_DIR=<dir> EC_PHASE=final bash pipeline/50_final_phase.sh data seed features members chain
# 3. the stream-transformer members, then the stackers again with their inputs and the tail, then the four post-steps
BND_NB=1 EC_PHASE=final bash pipeline/39_bnd1.sh members
BND_NB=1 EC_PHASE=final bash pipeline/39_bnd1.sh chain
BND_NB=1 EC_PHASE=final bash pipeline/39_post_steps.sh run
# 4. coverage gate; then the plan-window step, the template check and the file's sha256
EC_PHASE=final python scripts/final_coverage.py --require adsb2m
bash pipeline/40_final_file.sh outputs/overnight_final/test/ar_134naclh_tww.parquet

The published day-level traffic that the members read as features is not a switch: it is one name in three member scripts.

A departure with no ground track is handled by design: the stackers correct it from clocks and the chain's prediction, as they do at Istanbul and Paris, where open receivers hear almost nothing. A chain with no ADS-B input at all has no switch; docs/extending.md says which lines to edit and what it leaves out.

A feature family is one script that writes one file with a row per departure. Models and stackers find it by name. It must use only clocks, categories and other flights' records, and be built in every phase.

# the new family: overnight/features_rwy10.py writes data/processed[_<phase>]/features_dep_rwy10.parquet
# a member reads it by name (a new fit under outputs/overnight/oof), and its test-side refit follows
python -u mix.py --id my_member ... --extra ext_metar,ext_prc,ext_osm,ext_opdiev,ext_ord,rwy10
python -u final.py --from my_member --scale 1.15
# the month-complete stackers read it by name too
M12_EXTRA=adsbdir4,adsbrev,ef_10111040_stxctx_memb,ctx,ts_kdu_f,rwy10 bash pipeline/35_m12_stackers.sh x c l w avg
  • Without touching the blend: features_memb.py --all 1 --add <id> --out <name> lets the stackers see the new member's disagreement with the eleven, as the stream transformer does.
  • Into the blend: add its id to the list the blend reads, add its fit and refit lines to the member scripts of the stage and of every phase, and give the blend a new id.
